1The problem — audio is enormous, and every sample is entangled

A neural network that generates sound must learn a probability distribution $p(\mathbf{x})$ over waveforms $\mathbf{x}$ and then draw samples from it. That sounds like image generation, and much of the machinery is shared — but audio has two properties that make it uniquely hard.

First: the dimensionality is staggering

Compact-disc audio is 44,100 samples per second per channel. One second of mono audio is a point in a 44,100-dimensional space; a three-minute song lives in nearly eight million dimensions. Compare a $256\times256$ image at 65,536 pixels — a single second of speech at 16 kHz already exceeds a quarter of that, and songs dwarf it.

$$\dim(\text{1 s @ }16\text{ kHz}) = 16{,}000 \qquad \dim(\text{3 min song @ }44.1\text{ kHz, stereo}) \approx 2.4\times10^{7}$$

Second: the correlations span every time scale at once

In a spoken sentence, the sign of sample $n$ is set by the pitch period (a few milliseconds), the phoneme (tens of milliseconds), the word (hundreds), and the sentence prosody (seconds) — simultaneously. A model that captures pitch but not prosody produces robotic monotone; one that captures words but not pitch periods produces buzzing mush. This is the long-range-dependency problem, and it is the reason the field fractured into so many approaches.

Drag across the demo below to hear it. The same 0.4-second clip is generated three ways; watch how structure at one scale (the harmonic comb, the formant envelope, the decay) is a different-looking pattern in each domain — yet it is one sound.

Why not just predict the next sample and be done? You can — that is Part 2's WaveNet. At 16 kHz a one-second clip needs 16,000 sequential forward passes, and each must "remember" thousands of samples back to keep the pitch stable. Every technique in this series is, at heart, a different bargain with that arithmetic.

2Four domains — where a model chooses to work

You almost never generate raw samples directly from scratch. Instead you pick a representation that makes the distribution easier to learn, generate in that domain, and convert back. There are four dominant choices, in increasing order of abstraction.

DomainWhat it isDimensionality / stepWho works here

The trend of the last decade reads straight down that table: the field kept climbing the ladder of abstraction, letting a separately trained decoder shoulder the fidelity while the generative model works in an ever-friendlier space. The cost is a two-stage pipeline and a decoder that can hallucinate — a tension every later part returns to.

derivation · why the mel spectrogram is ~256× smaller yet keeps what matters

A mel spectrogram with STFT hop $H$ and $M$ mel bands stores $M$ numbers every $H$ samples, versus $H$ raw samples over the same span. The compression ratio is

$$\rho = \frac{H}{M}.$$

Typical speech settings $H=256,\,M=80$ give $\rho = 3.2$ per channel — but the raw signal also carries phase, which the magnitude spectrogram discards entirely. Counting the real+imaginary STFT that the magnitude replaces, a length-$N$ frame's $N$ real samples map to $N/2{+}1$ complex bins ($N{+}2$ reals) then collapse to $M$ magnitudes. With $N=1024$: $1024 \to 80$, about $13\times$ per frame, and because frames overlap by $H/N$ the effective per-sample saving compounds. The perceptual justification is that the mel warping and magnitude-only encoding discard exactly the information the ear is least sensitive to — absolute phase and fine high-frequency resolution — which is why a vocoder can plausibly invent it back (Part 3).

3Quantization — how autoregressive models turn a knob into a word

A model that predicts the next sample as a classification problem (WaveNet does exactly this) needs the continuous amplitude turned into a finite alphabet. Naive linear quantization wastes its bits: the ear hears loudness logarithmically, so quiet passages — where most of the signal lives — get almost no levels. The fix is μ-law companding, borrowed from 1960s telephony.

$$F(x) = \operatorname{sign}(x)\,\frac{\ln\!\left(1 + \mu\,|x|\right)}{\ln(1+\mu)},\qquad \mu = 255,\; x\in[-1,1]$$

Compress with $F$, quantize the result uniformly to 256 levels (8 bits), and WaveNet's original 16-bit-quality audio becomes a 256-way softmax — small enough to be tractable, and because the companding curve is steep near zero, the quiet detail survives.

Left: the companding curve. Right: quantization error on the signal. Drop to 3–4 bits and toggle μ-law to hear why telephony chose it.

Try this: set 3 bits and play with μ-law off — the quiet tail of the decaying tone turns to gravelly noise. Switch μ-law on at the same 3 bits: the tail is recognisable again. Same number of levels; smarter placement.

derivation · quantization noise and the 6 dB-per-bit rule

Uniform quantization to $b$ bits over a full-scale range $2A$ has step $\Delta = 2A/2^{b}$. Modelling the rounding error as uniform on $[-\Delta/2,\Delta/2]$ gives noise power

$$\sigma_q^2 = \frac{\Delta^2}{12} = \frac{A^2}{3\cdot 2^{2b}}.$$

For a full-scale sinusoid (signal power $A^2/2$) the signal-to-quantization-noise ratio is

$$\mathrm{SQNR} = 10\log_{10}\frac{A^2/2}{\sigma_q^2} = 6.02\,b + 1.76 \text{ dB}.$$

Each bit buys ~6 dB — but only on average across the range. The catch that μ-law fixes: a signal at $-40$ dB full scale only exercises the lowest few codes, so its effective $b$ collapses. Companding warps the step sizes to be small where the amplitude is small, holding SQNR roughly constant across loudness. That constant-SQNR property is exactly what a generative model wants: it must model quiet and loud passages with equal relative precision.

4Long context — the dilated-convolution trick

To keep pitch stable a waveform model must "see" thousands of past samples, but a plain stack of convolutions with kernel $k$ over $L$ layers only reaches back $L(k-1)+1$ samples — linearly. WaveNet's decisive idea was the dilated causal convolution: double the gap between kernel taps each layer, so the receptive field grows exponentially while the compute grows only linearly.

$$r = 1 + \sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}(k-1)\,d_\ell,\qquad d_\ell = 2^{\ell}\;\Rightarrow\; r = 1 + (k-1)\,(2^{L}-1)$$

With kernel $k=2$, ten layers reach back $2^{10}=1024$ samples; stack a few such blocks and you cover the hundreds of milliseconds a pitch contour needs — from a network only tens of layers deep. Drag the slider and watch the cone of dependency widen.

Each layer doubles the dilation. The lit edges trace which inputs the rightmost output actually depends on.
Exponential reach. Receptive field — — that many input samples feed the newest prediction, at a cost of only — layers.
The catch. This buys context, not speed. Generation is still one sample at a time: 16,000 forward passes per second. Parts 3, 8 and 9 are largely about escaping that sequential bottleneck.

5Five model families — five ways to place a distribution

Given a domain, how do you actually model $p(\mathbf{x})$ and sample from it? Five families dominate audio. Each is a different answer to the same trilemma — quality, sampling speed, and mode coverage (does it capture the full diversity of the data, or collapse to a few safe outputs?). No family wins all three; the history of the field is largely the search for better trade-offs.

GAN — a two-player minimax game with no likelihood at all:

$$\min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}_{x}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))].$$

Diffusion — learn the score / noise at every corruption level $t$:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,x_0,\,\epsilon}\big\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\; t)\big\|^2.$$

Flow — exact likelihood via the change-of-variables formula through an invertible $f$:

$$\log p(x) = \log p_z\!\big(f(x)\big) + \log\left|\det \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|.$$

VAE — maximise the evidence lower bound (reconstruction minus a KL prior):

$$\log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big).$$
Read the modern systems as combinations. VITS is a flow, a VAE, and a GAN at once. MusicGen is an autoregressive transformer over a VAE-like codec's tokens. Stable Audio is diffusion inside a VAE latent. The families are primitives; the art is in how you compose them.

6Evaluation — you cannot optimise what you cannot measure

Generated audio has no single "correct" target, so the field uses a ladder of metrics: subjective listening tests at the top, objective embedding distances below, and signal-level measures at the bottom. Every later part reports the ones that matter for its task.

Subjective — the ground truth

MOS: listeners rate naturalness 1–5; report the mean with a confidence interval. MUSHRA: a rigorous multi-stimulus test with a hidden reference and a hidden low-quality anchor, scored 0–100. Expensive and slow, but the only true measure of "does this sound real?"

Objective — the proxy

FAD (Fréchet Audio Distance) compares the distribution of generated vs real audio in a pretrained embedding space. PESQ and STOI score speech quality/intelligibility against a reference. CLAP score measures how well audio matches a text prompt.

$$\mathrm{FAD} = \lVert \mu_r - \mu_g \rVert_2^{2} + \operatorname{tr}\!\Big(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2\big(\Sigma_r \Sigma_g\big)^{1/2}\Big)$$

with $(\mu_r,\Sigma_r)$ and $(\mu_g,\Sigma_g)$ the mean and covariance of embeddings of a real and a generated set. It is the audio twin of the FID used for images: lower is better, and unlike a per-sample loss it rewards matching the corpus as a whole rather than any one target. Part 12 dissects FAD and CLAP in full.

Every objective metric is gameable. A model can score well on FAD and still sound wrong to a human; a vocoder can ace PESQ and buzz audibly. This is why the honest papers still run listening tests — and why the demos in this series ask you to be the judge.
